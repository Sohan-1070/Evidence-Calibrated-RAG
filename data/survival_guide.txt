ISLAND & WILDERNESS SURVIVAL HANDBOOK
======================================
A comprehensive offline knowledge base for survival scenarios.
Covers: Water, Food, Shelter, Fire, Medical, Navigation, Signaling, Mental Health, and more.

======================================
SECTION 1: WATER AND HYDRATION
======================================

Q: How do I find drinking water on an island?
A: Look for streams, rivers, or springs inland, especially flowing downhill from high ground. Follow birds or animal trails as they often lead to water. Collect morning dew by wiping leaves with a cloth and wringing it out. Dig at the base of cliffs, rock faces, or the inside bend of dry riverbeds where subsurface water collects. Rainwater collected from large leaves, tarps, or containers is safe to drink without purification.

Q: How do I purify water without a filter?
A: Boil water vigorously for at least 3 minutes to kill bacteria, viruses, and parasites. If you cannot boil, let water sit in a clear plastic bottle in direct sunlight for 6 hours (SODIS method). You can make a basic filter by layering grass, sand, and charcoal from your fire in a container with a hole at the bottom. This removes particles but does NOT kill germs, so always boil after filtering.

Q: Can I drink seawater?
A: NO. Never drink seawater. It contains 3.5% salt and will cause your kidneys to expel more water than you drink, accelerating dehydration and leading to death. If desperate, you can build a solar still to distill fresh water from saltwater using a plastic sheet and a container.

Q: How do I build a solar still for fresh water?
A: Dig a hole in the ground (2ft wide, 1ft deep). Place a container at the center. Optionally put salty/brackish water or green leaves in the hole to increase yield. Cover the hole with a clear plastic sheet, seal the edges with sand, and place a small rock in the center so the sheet dips toward the container. The sun evaporates water which condenses on the cool plastic and drips into the container. Produces 0.5 to 1 liter per day.

Q: How much water do I need per day to survive?
A: The minimum is about 1 liter (4 cups) per day in cool conditions. In hot tropical weather or if you are doing physical work, you need 2 to 3 liters. Signs of dehydration include dark urine, dizziness, headache, and dry mouth. Prioritize water over everything else. You can live 3 weeks without food but only 3 days without water.

Q: How do I collect rainwater on an island?
A: Tie large leaves or a tarp at an angle over a container. Stretch a shirt or clothing over a frame to act as a collection surface. Large tropical leaves like banana or taro leaves can funnel rain directly. Place containers in open clearings before a rain storm. Clean your collection surface first as it may have bird droppings. A 1-meter-square tarp can collect several liters in a moderate rain shower.

Q: Is coconut water safe to drink?
A: Yes. Coconut water from green (unripe) coconuts is sterile and nutritious, similar to oral rehydration fluid. Mature brown coconuts have less water but more fat-rich milk. To open a coconut without tools, find a sharp rock and strike the ridge line (equator) of the coconut repeatedly, rotating it, until it cracks open. Coconut water is an excellent source of electrolytes and hydration in a tropical survival scenario.

SECTION 2: FINDING AND EATING FOOD
======================================

Q: What can I eat on a tropical island?
A: Safe island food sources include: coconuts (fruit, water, and flesh), bananas and plantains, papayas, mangoes, figs, pandanus fruit, clams, oysters, mussels, crabs, fish, sea urchins, and snails. Avoid brightly colored frogs, fish with unusual colors or spines, and any plant with a milky white sap, as these are often toxic.

Q: How do I catch fish without fishing gear?
A: Build a fish trap by weaving sticks into a cone or basket shape with the opening facing downstream. Place it in a stream with the narrow end downstream. Fish swim in easily but cannot exit. You can also drive fish into a shallow pool by creating noise upstream and then grab them by hand. Create a spear by sharpening the end of a straight stick with a sharp rock. Stand still at the water's edge in early morning or evening when fish are near the surface.

Q: How do I know if a plant is safe to eat?
A: Use the Universal Edibility Test. First, rub the plant on your inner wrist and wait 15 minutes for a reaction. If no burning or itching occurs, place a small piece on your lips for 3 minutes. If safe, chew a small piece and hold it in your mouth without swallowing for 15 minutes. If no reaction, swallow and wait 8 hours. Only eat one new plant at a time. Avoid plants with: milky sap, almond scent, umbrella-shaped flowers, shiny leaves, or three-leafed patterns. Always prefer fruits that other animals (like birds and monkeys) are eating.

Q: How do I catch crabs and shellfish?
A: Crabs can be found under rocks in shallow tide pools, especially at low tide. Approach slowly, pin the crab from behind to avoid the claws, and grab the back of its shell. Shellfish such as mussels, oysters, and clams are found attached to rocks in the intertidal zone. Pry them off with a sharp stick or rock edge. Always cook shellfish thoroughly as they can carry bacteria. Do not eat shellfish that are already open before cooking (they may be dead and spoiled).

Q: How do I cook food without pots or pans?
A: Wrap food in large leaves (banana, taro) and place directly in hot coals. You can also cook fish or meat on a flat rock placed over the fire. Dig a pit, fill it with hot coals and rocks, layer food wrapped in leaves, and cover with soil for a slow earth oven cook (takes 2 to 4 hours). Hollow out green bamboo sections and fill with food and water to boil in the bamboo tube over fire. Skewer fish or meat on sticks and hold over flames.

Q: Can I eat insects on an island?
A: Yes. Insects are among the most calorie-dense foods available. Grasshoppers, crickets, ants, termites, beetles, and grubs are all safe when cooked. Remove wings and legs from grasshoppers. Cook all insects by roasting in the fire or boiling. Never eat brightly colored insects or those with a strong smell (they may be toxic). A handful of roasted termites provides significant protein and fat.

Q: How do I store food so it does not spoil?
A: Dry food in direct sunlight by cutting it into thin strips and hanging in a breezy, sunny location. This removes moisture and slows bacterial growth. Salt (from seawater) can be used to salt-cure fish or meat. Smoked food over a low fire also preserves it for several days. Cooked food spoils faster in tropical heat, so cook only what you can eat. Uncut fruits and coconuts in a shaded, cool area stay fresh for several days.

SECTION 3: BUILDING SHELTER
======================================

Q: How do I build an emergency shelter on an island?
A: Find two trees about 2 meters apart and tie a horizontal ridgepole between them about 1.5 meters high. Lean long sticks against both sides of the ridgepole to form an A-frame. Weave or layer large leaves (banana, palm, pandanus) starting from the bottom and overlapping like roof tiles so rain runs off. A good leaf-thatched shelter keeps you warm and dry and takes about 2 to 3 hours to build. Always build on high ground to avoid flooding during rain.

Q: Where should I set up camp on an island?
A: Choose high, flat ground away from the beach to avoid high tide flooding and wind. Stay near a freshwater source but not directly beside it (insects and flooding risk). Avoid dry riverbeds which can flash flood. Avoid large dead trees or branches overhead that could fall. Face your shelter entrance away from the prevailing wind direction. Proximity to the beach is useful for signaling, but sleep inland for warmth and safety.

Q: How do I stay warm on a cold island night?
A: Build a fire near the entrance of your shelter to radiate heat inside. Stuff your shelter walls and floor with dry leaves, grass, or palm fronds for insulation. Wear all your clothing, especially covering your neck, head, and extremities where heat escapes most. Huddle with other survivors if you are in a group. Building your shelter small reduces the air volume your body has to heat.

Q: How do I make a bed to sleep off the ground?
A: Sleeping on bare ground exposes you to cold, moisture, insects, and snakes. Build a simple platform bed by laying straight sticks or poles across two horizontal supports tied to four upright sticks. Layer palm fronds or large leaves on top. This keeps you 30 to 45 centimeters off the ground. Alternatively, weave a hammock from rope, vines, or strips of bark between two trees.

Q: How do I protect my shelter from rain?
A: Layer your roof covering starting from the bottom, overlapping each row like shingles on a roof so rain flows downward and off the edge. Use the widest, most waterproof leaves available (palm, banana, or large tropical leaves). Tie leaves to the frame with vines or strips of flexible bark. A well-thatched shelter of 3 to 4 layers of palm fronds can be completely waterproof in heavy rain.

SECTION 4: FIRE STARTING AND MANAGEMENT
======================================

Q: How do I start a fire without a lighter or matches?
A: Use the bow-drill method. Create a straight, dry spindle stick (about 50cm long), a flat fireboard with a small notch, a bow made from a curved stick with a shoelace or vine as the bowstring, and a small depression in a piece of bark to catch the coal. Wrap the bowstring around the spindle once, press the spindle into the fireboard notch, and move the bow back and forth rapidly while applying downward pressure. A hot coal will form in the notch. Transfer it to a tinder bundle of dry grass and gently blow until flame appears.

Q: How do I find good tinder to start a fire?
A: The best tinder is dry and fibrous. Look for dry grass, dry shredded bark (especially birch or dead coconut husks), dry leaves crumbled into a fine powder, dry animal fur or feathers, or the fluffy seed heads of plants. Tinder must be bone dry. In humid tropical conditions, look inside hollow logs or under rocks for dry material. Always have your tinder ready before creating your ember.

Q: How do I keep a fire burning through the night?
A: Add large, slow-burning hardwood logs to your fire before sleeping. Create a fire log: take two large pieces of wood and sandwich hot coals between them. This smolders for hours. You can also bank your fire by covering coals with ash, which insulates them and keeps them alive. In the morning, blow gently on the coals and add fresh tinder to revive the fire. Always maintain a dry supply of tinder and kindling near your camp.

Q: What wood is best for fire?
A: Use hardwoods like oak, coconut palm trunk, or any heavy, dense wood for long-burning fire with good heat. Use softwoods or dry grass for starting a fire quickly. Avoid green (living) wood as it produces excessive smoke and little heat. Avoid wood that smells strange or produces colored smoke as it may be toxic. Dead, dry standing wood is always better than wood on the ground (which absorbs moisture).

Q: How do I build a fire in the rain?
A: Find a sheltered spot like under a rock overhang, a dense tree canopy, or inside your shelter entrance. Collect tinder from inside your clothing where it has stayed dry against your body. Build a small platform of sticks first to keep your fire off the wet ground. Feather-stick tinder (shavings still attached to a stick) works well in damp conditions. Shield your growing flame with your body or a large leaf until it is strong enough to resist rain.

SECTION 5: FIRST AID AND MEDICAL EMERGENCIES
======================================

Q: How do I treat a jellyfish sting?
A: Rinse the sting area immediately with vinegar or seawater (NOT fresh water). Fresh water causes unfired stinging cells to release more venom and makes it worse. Remove visible tentacles with tweezers or a stick, never with bare hands. Immerse the area in hot water (as hot as bearable) for 20 minutes to break down venom proteins. Do not apply ice, urine, or meat tenderizer. For a box jellyfish (extremely dangerous), get to medical help immediately if possible as the venom can be fatal.

Q: How do I jump start a car?
A: Connect the red (positive) cable to the positive terminal (+) of the dead battery. Connect the other red clamp to the positive terminal (+) of the working battery. Connect the black (negative) cable to the negative terminal (-) of the working battery. Connect the final black clamp to an unpainted metal surface on the dead car's engine block (NOT the dead battery, to avoid sparks near the battery). Start the working car, let it run for 2 minutes, then start the dead car. Remove cables in reverse order.

Q: How do I treat a deep cut or wound in the wilderness?
A: Apply firm, direct pressure with the cleanest cloth available for at least 10 minutes without lifting to check. Do not remove a cloth that has soaked through; add more material on top. Elevate the injured limb above the heart if possible. Once bleeding stops, clean the wound with clean boiled water if available. Close the wound edges together with strips of cloth if it is a gaping cut. Do not use a tourniquet unless bleeding is life-threatening and cannot be controlled otherwise. Keep the wound covered and watch for infection (redness, warmth, pus, fever).

Q: How do I treat a sprained ankle in the wilderness?
A: Apply the RICE method: Rest (stop walking on it), Ice (cool it with wet cloth or seawater if no ice), Compression (wrap it firmly with a bandage or strips of cloth), Elevation (prop the foot above heart level). Do not try to push through severe ankle pain. Fashion a walking stick to reduce weight on the ankle. Wrap the ankle firmly with cloth for stability if you must walk. Swelling and bruising are normal; inability to put any weight on it could indicate a fracture.

Q: How do I remove a fish hook from skin?
A: If the barb has not passed through the skin, back the hook out the same way it went in. If the barb is embedded, push the hook forward until the barb exits the skin, then cut the barb off with pliers or a sharp rock and pull the shank back out. Clean the wound thoroughly with boiled water afterward. Watch for infection in the following days.

Q: How do I treat a fever without medicine?
A: Keep the person cool by removing excess clothing, applying cool damp cloths to the forehead, armpits, and groin. Ensure the person drinks plenty of fluids. If the person is shivering, do not try to warm them excessively (shivering is the body generating heat to fight infection). A high fever (above 39°C or 102°F) that doesn't reduce, or is accompanied by confusion, stiff neck, or rash, is a medical emergency requiring evacuation.

Q: How do I treat sunburn on an island?
A: Get out of the sun immediately. Apply cool (not cold) water or wet cloth to burned areas. Aloe vera gel from the inner leaf of a succulent plant soothes burns. Drink extra water as sunburn draws fluid to the skin surface. Do not pop blisters. Cover burned areas with loose, breathable cloth when in the sun. Severe sunburn with blistering over large areas can cause infection and should be treated as a serious wound.

Q: How do I treat dehydration?
A: The person needs fluid immediately. Give them clean water in small, frequent sips rather than large amounts at once. If available, add a small pinch of salt and a teaspoon of sugar to the water to make an oral rehydration solution. Have the person rest in shade. Signs of severe dehydration include confusion, rapid heartbeat, not urinating, and sunken eyes. This requires large amounts of fluid urgently. Coconut water is an excellent natural rehydration fluid.

Q: How do I treat a coral cut?
A: Coral cuts are notorious for becoming infected because coral fragments get embedded in the wound. Scrub the cut gently but thoroughly with clean water and a clean cloth to remove all coral fragments. Clean with antiseptic if available. Do not cover tightly as coral cuts need to breathe and dry. Keep inspecting daily for signs of infection (redness spreading outward, warmth, pus, or fever). Coral cuts that look minor can become serious infections within days in tropical environments.

Q: How do I treat heat stroke?
A: Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency where the body temperature rises above 40°C and the body cannot cool itself. Move the person immediately into shade. Remove excess clothing. Cool the person rapidly using any available method: pour water over them, fan them vigorously, apply wet cloths to armpits, groin, and neck where large blood vessels are close to the surface. Give fluids if the person is conscious and able to swallow. Do not give aspirin or paracetamol (they don't work for heat stroke). The goal is to cool the body as fast as possible.

Q: What do I do if someone stops breathing?
A: Check the airway, tilt the head back, lift the chin, and look, listen, and feel for breathing. If not breathing, start CPR. Give 30 chest compressions in the center of the chest, pressing down at least 5cm at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute. Give 2 rescue breaths by pinching the nose and breathing into the mouth. Continue cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths until the person breathes on their own or help arrives.

Q: How do I make a splint for a broken bone?
A: Do not try to straighten the bone. Pad the area with soft cloth or leaves. Find two straight, rigid sticks long enough to extend beyond the joints above and below the break. Place the sticks on either side of the broken limb. Tie them securely above and below the break with strips of cloth, but not directly on the break itself. The goal is to immobilize the joint so the bone cannot move. Check regularly that the ties are not cutting off circulation (fingers or toes should remain warm and the person should be able to wriggle them).

SECTION 6: SIGNALING AND RESCUE
======================================

Q: How do I signal for rescue on an island?
A: The universal distress signal is SOS. Use three of anything: three fires, three blasts of a whistle, three shouts. Signal fire: Build a large smoky fire on the highest point or beach visible from the sea and air. Use green leaves to create thick white smoke (visible against dark jungle). Mirror signaling: Use a shiny piece of metal, glass, or foil to reflect sunlight. A mirror reflection can be seen 10 to 16 kilometers away by aircraft. In a clearing, lay out large rocks or logs in the shape of SOS or V (request assistance) or X (need medical help).

Q: What colors are most visible for a signal?
A: International Air/Ground signals use specific colors. Bright orange, red, and pink are most visible from the air. If you have a bright-colored tent, tarp, or clothing, lay it in a clearing visible from above. Bright colors against the natural green of an island jungle are highly visible to aircraft. At night, signal fires are extremely visible from the air.

Q: How do I signal aircraft flying overhead?
A: Use a mirror or foil to flash reflected sunlight in the direction of the aircraft. Use a signal fire with lots of green-leaf smoke during the day, or large bright flames at night. Move to the most visible clearing you can find. Wave a bright cloth overhead in a slow, deliberate figure-eight pattern (not a fast wave, which looks like someone waving hello). Three fires in a triangle is a universally recognized distress signal.

Q: How do I signal passing ships?
A: A mirror or any reflective surface is very effective during the day. Signal fires on the beach with heavy smoke during daylight and bright flames at night. A large SOS written in the sand or rocks, at least 10 meters tall to be visible from a ship. Use a whistle or loud sounds if a boat comes close. Bright-colored clothing waved from the highest point visible from the sea can also attract attention.

Q: What does SOS mean and how do I signal it in Morse code?
A: SOS is the international distress signal. In Morse code it is three dots, three dashes, three dots (... --- ...). You can signal it with a mirror flash (short-short-short, long-long-long, short-short-short), with whistle blasts, with light from a torch, or by tapping. It does not stand for anything specific; it was chosen because it is easy to recognize and remember.

SECTION 7: NAVIGATION
======================================

Q: How do I find north without a compass?
A: Sun method: In the northern hemisphere, the sun rises slightly north of east and sets slightly north of west. At noon, it is roughly due south. Shadow stick method: Push a stick into the ground and mark the tip of its shadow. Wait 15 minutes and mark the new shadow tip. A line drawn from the first mark to the second mark runs roughly east to west. Stars: Find the Southern Cross (visible in tropical regions) and extend the long axis 4.5 times beyond the foot of the cross to find south. In the northern hemisphere, find Polaris (the North Star) which is always due north.

Q: How do I navigate using the stars?
A: In the Southern Hemisphere (where many tropical islands are), find the Southern Cross (Crux) constellation, a group of 4 bright stars forming a kite shape. Extend the long axis of the cross by 4.5 times in the direction the cross is "pointing" to find the South Celestial Pole, which indicates due south on the horizon. In the Northern Hemisphere, find Polaris (North Star) by drawing a line through the two stars at the end of the Big Dipper's bowl - they point directly to Polaris, which is always due north.

Q: How do I know which direction the wind is coming from?
A: Wet your finger and hold it up. The side that feels cold indicates the direction the wind is coming FROM. Tropical trade winds typically blow from the east. If you can observe clouds or waves, they generally move in the direction of wind flow. Knowing wind direction helps with fire management (build fire so smoke blows away from camp) and navigation (consistent wind patterns mean consistent direction).

Q: How do I estimate how far I have traveled?
A: Count your paces (double steps). An average adult takes approximately 1,000 double steps per kilometer on flat ground. On rough terrain, divide your estimate by 2. You can also time your travel: walking at a steady pace covers approximately 4 to 5 kilometers per hour on flat ground, 2 to 3 kilometers per hour in dense terrain. Mark your path with sticks or rocks so you can find your way back to camp.

SECTION 8: MENTAL HEALTH AND GROUP SURVIVAL
======================================

Q: How do I stay mentally strong in a survival situation?
A: Set small, achievable goals each day (collect water, build fire, find food). Having a purpose and tasks prevents panic and despair. Maintain a daily routine as routine provides psychological comfort. Stay positive about rescue; most survival situations are resolved within days to weeks. Keep busy with tasks. If you have other survivors, talk to them and share responsibilities. Practice gratitude for small successes. Panic is the biggest killer in survival situations: it causes bad decisions and wasted energy.

Q: How do I manage a group of survivors on an island?
A: Assign clear roles and tasks: someone for water, fire, food, and signaling. Establish a leader by group consensus to prevent conflict. Make group decisions together when possible, but have a final decision-maker. Rotate uncomfortable tasks. Check on everyone's physical and mental health daily. Conflict in a survival situation is extremely dangerous; resolve disagreements quickly and calmly. Together you have more skills, more energy, and a better chance of survival than alone.

Q: How do I deal with fear and panic in a survival situation?
A: Control your breathing. Take slow, deep breaths (breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4) to activate the body's calm response. Accept that fear is normal and useful. Redirect fear into productive action: panic attacks waste energy; identify one concrete task to do right now. Talk to yourself or others out loud. Write a plan or a list. Focus on the present: what do you need in the next hour, not in the next month. Remember that humans are remarkably resilient and survival stories are more common than you think.

Q: How do I stay motivated when rescue seems unlikely?
A: Maintain hope but plan for long-term survival. People have survived months on remote islands. Calculate your resources and be methodical. Think of your family and people waiting for you. Set a goal like "I will be rescued by [date]" but keep working as if you might stay longer. Celebrate every small success: a good fire, a full water container, a meal. Write a journal if you have materials; it organizes thoughts and creates purpose. Accept the situation without catastrophizing about it.

SECTION 9: TOOLS AND IMPROVISED EQUIPMENT
======================================

Q: How do I make a knife from a rock?
A: Find a piece of flint, obsidian, or any fine-grained hard rock. Use another hard rock as a hammerstone and strike glancing blows at the edge of your flint at about 45 degrees to chip off flakes. Work toward the center to thin the stone. The resulting edge can be razor-sharp. Attach the stone to a wooden handle using cord or strips of bark wrapped tightly. Knapping (stone tool making) takes practice, but even a sharp-edged rock fragment is extremely useful.

Q: How do I make rope from natural materials?
A: Find long, fibrous plant material: palm leaves, tall grass, strips of inner bark from dead trees, or vines. Divide the fibres into two bundles. Twist each bundle tightly clockwise. Twist the two bundles together counter-clockwise. This opposing twist locks them together and prevents unraveling. The more fibres you use, the thicker and stronger the rope. Natural rope will weaken if wet so always make more than you need.

Q: How do I make a container to carry and boil water?
A: A thick green bamboo segment (sealed at both ends by bamboo nodes) can hold water and be placed directly over a fire to boil water. Large tropical leaves can be folded and pinned with thorns into a cone shape for carrying water short distances. You can also dig a hole and line it with a plastic bag, large leaf, or animal skin to create a small reservoir. Coconut shells make excellent cups and bowls.

Q: How do I make a fishing line and hook?
A: Line: Use thin, strong natural fibre rope. Vines, thin roots, or strips of inner bark twisted into a cord work well. Hook: Find a thin, hard piece of wood or bone and carve a sharp curved hook shape. A thorn can be tied to a short line as a hook. A gorge hook (a straight sharp point tied in the middle of the line, baited so a fish swallows it sideways) is one of the oldest and simplest fishing tools.

Q: How do I make a torch to see at night?
A: Find a dry stick about 60cm long. Wrap one end tightly with dry fibrous material: dry grass, strips of bark, dry leaves, or coconut husk fibre. Tie it firmly with a vine or cord. Soak the wrapped end in tree resin, rendered animal fat, or coconut oil if available. Light the end. This will burn for 10 to 20 minutes. Always have a second torch prepared. Keep your primary fire burning at camp so you can relight your torch and have light to navigate back.

SECTION 10: ISLAND-SPECIFIC HAZARDS
======================================

Q: What animals should I avoid on a tropical island?
A: Avoid: cone snails (their sting delivers venom that can kill within hours, do not touch any cone-shaped snail shells), stonefish (camouflaged as rocks in shallow water, their dorsal spines deliver potent venom; always look before placing feet in water), sea snakes (generally docile but highly venomous if provoked), centipedes (painful sting, rarely fatal), and unfamiliar spiders. Give all wildlife space and do not provoke or handle anything you cannot identify.

Q: How do I treat a stonefish sting?
A: This is a medical emergency. Immerse the affected limb in the hottest water you can tolerate (up to 45°C) for at least 30 minutes. Heat breaks down the venom's proteins and provides significant pain relief. Remove visible spines with tweezers if possible. The pain is described as the worst of any animal sting in the world. Seek medical evacuation urgently as anti-venom may be required. Without treatment, severe stings can be fatal within hours.

Q: What plants should I avoid on a tropical island?
A: Avoid: any plant with white or milky sap, plants that smell like almonds (often contain cyanide compounds), plants with umbrella-shaped flowers, plants with glossy berries that you cannot identify (many are toxic), and the manchineel tree (bright green apples, standing under it in rain causes skin burns, eating its fruit is potentially fatal). If you are unsure about a plant, do not eat it and use the Universal Edibility Test.

Q: How do I avoid getting lost in island jungle?
A: Always mark your path when exploring inland using broken branches, small piles of rocks, or strips of cloth tied to trees. Move from high point to high point so you can orient yourself. The beach and the sound of waves are always a navigation reference on an island. Keep the sun's position in mind as a compass reference. If you realize you are lost, stop moving. Stay calm. Backtrack along your marked trail. Shouting and making noise will help rescuers find you.

Q: How do I protect myself from insects and mosquitoes?
A: Cover exposed skin with clothing, especially at dawn and dusk when mosquitoes are most active. Apply mud or ash to exposed skin as a natural repellent. Sleep inside your shelter with the entrance covered. Burn green leaves or certain aromatic plants (like citronella grass or eucalyptus leaves) near your camp to deter insects. Mosquitoes in tropical regions can carry malaria, dengue, and other diseases. Protect yourself consistently from the very first day.

Q: What do I do if I suspect I have malaria symptoms?
A: Malaria symptoms typically appear 10 to 15 days after a mosquito bite and include high fever with cycles of cold, shaking, then sweating, severe headache, muscle aches, fatigue, and vomiting. Without anti-malarial medication, prioritize keeping the fever down (cool water, shade), maintaining hydration, and rest. This is a medical emergency requiring evacuation. Prevention through insect bite avoidance is critical in known malaria zones.

Q: What do I do if I step on a sea urchin?
A: Remove spines carefully with tweezers if they are accessible. Spines that break off and remain in the skin can work their way deeper. Soak the affected area in hot water to soften skin and reduce pain. Some recommend soaking in vinegar to dissolve remaining spine fragments. Do not try to dig out deeply embedded spines in a survival scenario as this can cause more damage. Keep the wound clean and watch for infection.
